After a storm, or before you buy a house, the question is usually the same: what's actually going on up there? A drone can show you in under an hour, without anyone climbing on the roof.

## What I photograph

Every roof check follows the same order, so nothing gets missed:

1. **The overview.** The whole roof from about 30 metres up: every slope, the chimney, any extensions and the gutters in one frame.
2. **Every slope, square on.** Close enough to count the tiles or slates, so anything slipped, cracked or missing stands out.
3. **The ridge and hips.** A slow pass along the top, where ridge tiles and mortar take the worst of the weather.
4. **The chimney.** Every side of the stack, the pots from above and the lead flashing where it meets the roof. It's the hardest part to see from the ground.
5. **Gutters and flat roofs.** Looking down into the gutters, not up at them, and flat roofs from directly above, where standing water and splits are easy to spot.

## What you get

One pack of photos, by download link, within 48 to 72 hours. Each close-up is numbered, and the numbers are marked on the overview so you can see exactly where each photo was taken.

You can forward it straight to a roofer, a surveyor, your landlord or your insurer. Clear photos help a roofer give you an accurate quote, and help an insurer decide on a claim.

## Why not a ladder or scaffolding?

A ladder only reaches the gutters, and it's risky without the right training and kit. Scaffolding gets you close, but it takes days to arrange and can cost hundreds of pounds a week for a chimney alone. A drone gets close-up photos of the whole roof in one visit, and nobody has to go up.

## What it doesn't do

I'm not a roofer, so I don't diagnose faults or tell you what a repair should cost. The photos show what's there; your roofer or surveyor decides what it means.

It also isn't a replacement for a building survey when you're buying. It's the close-up roof photos to put in front of the person doing the survey.

## Who it's for

Roof checks get booked for very different reasons. Here's who uses them most, and what each one gets out of it.

### Homeowners after a storm

High winds lift tiles, ridge caps and lead, and from the garden it can be impossible to tell what's moved. A roof check shows you exactly what's happened before you pay anyone to go up, so you know whether you need a roofer this week or not at all.

If there is damage, you've got clear, dated photos to send to roofers for quotes, so they can price the job without each one climbing up. You don't usually need to be in for the flight.

### Buyers, before you commit

The roof is one of the most expensive things to get wrong on a house purchase, and surveyors often can't see much of it from the ground. Close-up photos of every slope, the chimney and the flat roofs give your surveyor, or a roofer you trust, something real to look at before you exchange.

It doesn't replace a survey; it fills the gap where the survey says "roof not inspected at close quarters".

### Roofers: quoting without the climb

Every enquiry you go up a ladder for takes time, and some roofs are too high, steep or fragile to get on safely without access equipment. Photos of the whole roof let you price the job accurately from the van or the office, and turn down the ones that aren't worth it.

Regular work can be invoiced monthly instead of per roof.

### Roofers and builders: before and after

Dated photos of the roof before you start and after you finish protect you if a customer later says something was damaged, and show off the finished job. The after shots make good marketing for your own website and social media.

### Landlords: a yearly record

A small problem left for a winter becomes a big one: a blocked gutter becomes damp, a slipped tile becomes a leak. A check once or twice a year gives you a dated record of every roof in your portfolio, so small jobs get done while they're still small. Quarterly or yearly visits come with a discount on repeat bookings.

### Property managers: blocks and estates

Every roof on a block, a row of shops or a whole estate, recorded on a schedule and named by building. It means you can see the condition of the whole estate without arranging access to every roof, and plan maintenance budgets from photos instead of guesswork.

### Insurance claims

When something's gone wrong, insurers and loss adjusters need evidence. Clear, dated photos of the damage, taken from every angle, help your claim along. Next-day delivery is available when you need the photos quickly. Your insurer still decides the claim.

### Solar installers

See the roof, its condition, the shading and the layout before you quote, so there are no surprises on install day. After the install, a shot of the finished array is the best photo you'll have of the job for the customer and your marketing.

### Churches, schools and other large buildings

Tall, steep or fragile roofs are where scaffolding costs the most and takes longest to arrange. These flights are planned with the site in advance, with the airspace checked and a written plan agreed before the day.
